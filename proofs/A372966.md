# A372966: sigma_8(n^2) / sigma_4(n^2) is congruent to 1 modulo 240

## Statement

OEIS [A372966](https://oeis.org/A372966) is
$a(n) = \sigma_8(n^2)/\sigma_4(n^2)$, where $\sigma_k(m)$ is the sum of the
k-th powers of the divisors of m. A comment in the entry says:

> Apparently, a(n) == 1 (mod 240).

Status on 2026-11-26: listed as an observation without proof (since 2024).

**Theorem.** $a(n) \equiv 1 \pmod{240}$ for every $n \ge 1$. The modulus 240
is the largest one with this property, because $a(2) = 241$.

## Prior work

Checked on 2026-11-27. The OEIS entry still shows the statement as
"Apparently". A literature search found no proof.

The result is a short consequence of known facts. The entry already records
the prime-power formula $a(p^e) = (p^{8e+4}+1)/(p^4+1)$, and
$x^8 \equiv x^4 \pmod{240}$ holds for all integers x (Lemma 1 below). This note
writes the argument down in full.

## Proof

**Lemma 1.** For every integer x and every $i \ge 1$,
$x^{4i} \equiv x^4 \pmod{240}$.

*Proof.* It is enough to show $x^8 \equiv x^4$, that is
$240 \mid x^4 (x^4 - 1)$; the general case follows by induction on i, since
$x^{4(i+1)} = x^{4i} x^4 \equiv x^8 \equiv x^4$. As $240 = 16 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$
with pairwise coprime factors, we check each factor.

- Modulo 16: if x is even then $16 \mid x^4$. If x is odd then
  $x^2 = 1 + 8u$ for some integer u, so $x^4 = 1 + 16u + 64u^2 \equiv 1$.
- Modulo 3: either $3 \mid x$ or $x^2 \equiv 1$, so $x^4 \equiv 1$.
- Modulo 5: either $5 \mid x$ or $x^4 \equiv 1$ by Fermat's little theorem.

In every case the factor divides $x^4$ or $x^4 - 1$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.** The function a is multiplicative, and for a prime p and
$e \ge 0$,

```math
a(p^e) = \sum_{i=0}^{2e} (-1)^i\, p^{4i} .
```

*Proof.* The functions $n \mapsto \sigma_8(n^2)$ and $n \mapsto \sigma_4(n^2)$
are multiplicative and positive, so their quotient is multiplicative. Put
$y = p^4$. Then

```math
\sigma_4(p^{2e}) = \frac{y^{2e+1}-1}{y-1}, \qquad
\sigma_8(p^{2e}) = \frac{y^{2(2e+1)}-1}{y^2-1},
```

and the quotient is

```math
\frac{y^{2e+1}+1}{y+1} = \sum_{i=0}^{2e} (-y)^i ,
```

a polynomial identity because $2e+1$ is odd. $\square$

The prime-power formula is equivalent to the one recorded in the formula
section of the entry since 2024, $a(p^e) = (p^{8e+4}+1)/(p^4+1)$.

**Proof of the theorem.** By Lemma 1, $p^{4i} \equiv p^4 \pmod{240}$ for
$i \ge 1$, so by Lemma 2

```math
a(p^e) \equiv 1 + p^4 \sum_{i=1}^{2e} (-1)^i = 1 \pmod{240},
```

because the sum has e terms equal to $-1$ and e terms equal to $+1$. By
multiplicativity a(n) is a product of such values, hence
$a(n) \equiv 1 \pmod{240}$. Finally $a(2) = 1 - 16 + 256 = 241$, so no larger
modulus works for all n. $\blacksquare$

## Remarks

1. Lemma 1 holds for all integers, so primality is used only through
   multiplicativity.
2. The same argument gives: if M is a positive integer with
   $x^{2k} \equiv x^k \pmod M$ for all integers x, then
   $\sigma_{2k}(n^2)/\sigma_k(n^2) \equiv 1 \pmod M$ for all n.

## Verification

`verify/a372966.py` checks Lemma 1 for all residues modulo 240, and checks
the exact divisibility, the first terms against the OEIS data and the
congruence for all n <= 20000. For n <= 400 the divisor sums are also
computed directly from the divisors of n^2, without using multiplicativity.

## Formalization

`OeisProofs/A372966.lean` contains a complete Lean 4 proof. The final theorem
is

```lean
theorem quotient_mod (n : Nat) (hn : Ne n 0) :
    Dvd.dvd (sigma 4 (n ^ 2)) (sigma 8 (n ^ 2)) /\
      sigma 8 (n ^ 2) / sigma 4 (n ^ 2) % 240 = 1
```

(the same statement as in the Lean file, written with ASCII spellings;
`sigma` is Mathlib's `ArithmeticFunction.sigma`). It states that the quotient is an integer and is
congruent to 1 modulo 240. The theorem `quotient_two` is the evaluation
a(2) = 241, which gives the optimality of 240. The proofs depend only on
the standard axioms `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`.
